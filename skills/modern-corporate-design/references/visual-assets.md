# Modern Corporate / Expressive Minimalism Visual Assets Guide

> A brand-neutral art-direction and graphic-system reference for modern corporate websites.
>
> This document covers photography, video, illustration, icons, diagrams, charts, borders, separators, shapes, patterns, textures, badges, visual motifs, responsive delivery, accessibility, and asset governance.

---

## 1. Purpose

A corporate website needs a coherent visual-assets system, not a collection of unrelated images and icons.

The visual system must:

- explain the business;
- establish trust;
- create recognition;
- support hierarchy;
- clarify processes;
- demonstrate real capability;
- differentiate the brand;
- remain accessible and performant.

Every visual element must be assigned a role.

---

## 2. Visual hierarchy

Use four levels:

1. **Evidence visuals**  
   Real work, product, environment, people, outcomes, data.

2. **Explanatory visuals**  
   Diagrams, process flows, charts, maps, interface views, technical illustrations.

3. **Navigational visuals**  
   Icons, arrows, status marks, controls, indicators.

4. **Atmospheric visuals**  
   Texture, pattern, abstract form, background video, decorative geometry.

Priority order:

```text
Evidence > explanation > navigation > atmosphere
```

Atmospheric graphics must never overwhelm evidence or content.

---

## 3. Art-direction principles

## 3.1 Specific over generic

Use visuals that could plausibly belong only to the company, industry, capability, or customer problem.

Prefer:

- actual facilities;
- actual teams;
- actual products;
- real customer environments;
- real processes;
- real interfaces;
- documented outcomes;
- relevant materials and details.

Avoid generic imagery that could represent almost any company.

## 3.2 One visual language

Define one dominant direction:

- documentary corporate;
- editorial corporate;
- technical precision;
- premium architectural;
- people-centered;
- bold graphic;
- data-led;
- product-led.

Supporting visual types must fit this direction.

## 3.3 Restraint with signature

Create distinction through one or two repeatable devices:

- a specific crop;
- a colored edge;
- numbered image captions;
- a custom icon stroke;
- a geometric mask;
- a line motif;
- a data-label system;
- monochrome images with one signal color;
- a consistent diagram language.

Do not invent a new graphic treatment per section.

## 3.4 Function defines treatment

Hero photography may be expressive. Product documentation, safety information, maps, and charts require higher fidelity and less stylization.

## 5. Photography

## 5.1 Documentary corporate photography

Best for:

- industrial companies;
- logistics;
- engineering;
- professional operations;
- healthcare;
- services;
- employer branding.

Characteristics:

- real environments;
- natural behavior;
- credible work activity;
- context visible;
- controlled but not over-staged;
- realistic color;
- clear focal subject;
- space for layout where required.

## 5.2 Editorial corporate photography

Best for:

- consulting;
- architecture;
- finance;
- premium services;
- leadership;
- thought leadership.

Characteristics:

- deliberate composition;
- stronger crop;
- controlled lighting;
- visual negative space;
- architectural lines;
- confident portraits;
- higher contrast;
- selective use of monochrome.

## 5.3 Technical and industrial photography

Show:

- scale;
- material;
- machinery;
- process;
- safety;
- precision;
- environment;
- human interaction;
- product details.

Rules:

- Include wide, medium, and detail views.
- Avoid images dominated by irrelevant safety gear or machinery when the actual service is unclear.
- Keep branding and confidential data under control.
- Do not misrepresent unsafe work practices.
- Maintain accurate color where product identification matters.

## 5.4 Human-centered photography

Show people as active participants, not decoration.

Prefer:

- real interaction;
- work in context;
- varied roles;
- natural expression;
- credible wardrobe;
- realistic environments;
- representative casting.

Avoid:

- staged handshakes;
- synchronized laughter;
- pointing at blank screens;
- people used only to fill a layout;
- artificial diversity without credible context;
- anonymous silhouettes when trust requires real people.

## 5.5 Portraits

Define:

- camera height;
- lens feel;
- crop;
- background;
- lighting;
- expression;
- wardrobe guidance;
- color treatment;
- aspect ratio.

Approved portrait sets should look related.

Recommended crops:

```text
3:4 or 4:5 leadership portrait
1:1 directory thumbnail
16:9 or 3:2 editorial context portrait
```

## 5.6 Product photography

Use:

- neutral documentation view;
- contextual use view;
- detail view;
- scale view;
- optional exploded or annotated view.

Do not stylize product images in ways that alter:

- color;
- material;
- included components;
- dimensions;
- condition;
- safety information.

## 5.7 Case-study imagery

A case-study image set should include:

1. context;
2. challenge;
3. work or intervention;
4. detail;
5. outcome;
6. people where relevant.

One generic cover image is not sufficient for a strong case study.

---

## 6. Stock photography policy

Stock imagery may be used when original production is unavailable, but it must pass a relevance test.

Reject images with:

- generic office posing;
- implausible teams;
- fake meetings;
- excessive digital overlays;
- obvious model behavior;
- meaningless city skylines;
- unrelated industrial scenes;
- incorrect equipment;
- artificial “innovation” graphics;
- recognizable overused stock compositions.

Select stock with:

- believable context;
- correct industry details;
- natural lighting;
- room for layout;
- consistent color and lens language;
- clear licensing.

Never imply that stock subjects are employees, customers, facilities, or completed projects.

---

## 7. Image color treatment

## 7.1 Natural corporate

- accurate color;
- restrained contrast;
- neutral whites;
- realistic skin;
- controlled saturation.

## 7.2 Editorial neutral

- slightly reduced saturation;
- stronger tonal separation;
- subtle cool or warm bias;
- consistent black point;
- clean highlights.

## 7.3 Monochrome plus signal color

Use when:

- brand identity is strong;
- archive and modernity must coexist;
- photography varies in source quality;
- a consistent editorial system is needed.

Rules:

- Keep subject separation.
- Do not use the signal color indiscriminately.
- Preserve accessible contrast for overlays.
- Avoid making all people appear emotionally distant.

## 7.4 Brand-color overlay

Use sparingly for:

- section transitions;
- secondary imagery;
- background imagery;
- campaign moments.

Do not place a heavy overlay over evidence that needs accurate visual interpretation.

---

## 8. Image composition

## 8.1 Negative space

Plan negative space during production. Do not assume text can be placed over any image.

Specify:

- text-left;
- text-right;
- text-top;
- no overlay;
- centered subject;
- edge subject;
- full-bleed detail.

## 8.2 Crop system

Define standard ratios:

```text
Hero: 16:9, 3:2, or custom wide
Feature: 4:3 or 3:2
Card: 4:3, 3:2, or 1:1
Portrait: 4:5 or 3:4
Logo field: fixed safe-area box
Social: 1.91:1 and 1:1
```

Use fewer ratios for stronger consistency.

## 8.3 Art-directed responsive crops

Desktop and mobile often require different compositions.

Mobile crop should:

- preserve focal subject;
- remove nonessential empty areas;
- keep faces and products intact;
- support relocated text;
- avoid accidental awkward crops;
- remain useful without the desktop context.

---

## 9. Illustration

## 9.1 When to use illustration

Use illustration to:

- explain an abstract service;
- visualize an invisible process;
- show a future state;
- simplify complex systems;
- add personality where photography cannot;
- create proprietary visual language.

Do not use generic illustration merely because a section lacks imagery.

## 9.2 Approved corporate illustration directions

### Geometric editorial

- simple shapes;
- limited palette;
- clear hierarchy;
- asymmetric composition;
- high scalability.

### Technical line illustration

- precise stroke;
- controlled detail;
- annotation;
- engineering or process context.

### Human hand-drawn accent

- warm;
- restrained;
- used around real content;
- not childish unless the brand requires it.

### Isometric system illustration

- useful for infrastructure and process;
- must be spatially consistent;
- avoid generic “floating tech city” clichés.

### Data-informed illustration

- based on real relationships;
- combines diagram and editorial composition;
- avoids fake charts.

## 9.3 Illustration rules

- Use one family.
- Define stroke weights.
- Define corner treatment.
- Define fill logic.
- Define perspective.
- Define human proportions.
- Define detail level by size.
- Use SVG where practical.
- Do not mix flat, 3D, hand-drawn, and isometric styles without a system.

## 10. Icon system

Icons are functional symbols first and brand assets second.

## 10.1 Icon categories

- interface icons;
- navigation icons;
- service icons;
- industry icons;
- status icons;
- social icons;
- file-type icons;
- data and map icons;
- decorative feature symbols.

## 10.2 Icon anatomy

Define:

```text
Base grid: 16, 20, 24, or 32 units
Stroke weight
Stroke caps
Stroke joins
Corner radius
Optical size
Filled or outlined style
Default bounding box
Safe area
```

Recommended common UI grid:

```text
24 × 24 viewBox
1.5–2 unit stroke
round or square cap selected globally
consistent optical alignment
```

## 10.3 Icon rules

- Use a single icon family for core UI.
- Do not mix outline and filled icons arbitrarily.
- Keep icons visually balanced, not merely mathematically centered.
- Pair unfamiliar icons with labels.
- Do not use icons as decoration inside every heading.
- Avoid icons in colored circles as a universal card formula.
- Use arrows consistently.
- Use SVG, not icon fonts, where practical.
- Ensure stroke and color meet non-text contrast requirements when essential.
- Decorative icons should be hidden from assistive technology.
- Functional icons need accessible names.

## 10.4 Service icons

A custom service-icon system should be:

- conceptually specific;
- simple enough to recognize;
- visually consistent;
- usable at 24–64 px;
- not a substitute for a service title.

Avoid vague icons such as:

- lightbulb for innovation;
- rocket for growth;
- puzzle pieces for solutions;
- handshake for partnership;
- globe for global;

unless context makes them meaningful.

## 10.5 Icon containers

Allowed:

- no container;
- square tile;
- subtle outlined box;
- partial corner frame;
- small color field;
- brand-shaped mask.

Use one system. Do not alternate circles, rounded squares, pills, and free icons without purpose.

---

## 11. Borders

Borders establish structure and may carry brand character.

## 11.1 Border roles

- section boundary;
- card boundary;
- data grouping;
- input affordance;
- image frame;
- emphasis;
- navigation state;
- decorative motif.

## 11.2 Border system

Define:

```css
--border-subtle: 1px solid var(--color-border-subtle);
--border-default: 1px solid var(--color-border);
--border-strong: 2px solid var(--color-text);
--border-accent: 2px solid var(--color-brand);
```

## 11.3 Rules

- Do not use a border if spacing already creates sufficient grouping.
- Do not border every section and every card.
- Use strong borders for emphasis, not routine containment.
- Inputs need a perceivable boundary unless another clear visual indicator is used.
- Keep border contrast sufficient for meaningful UI boundaries.
- Use partial borders only when component state remains clear.
- Avoid hairlines that disappear on low-quality displays.

---

## 12. Separators and dividers

Separators should communicate hierarchy, sequence, or grouping.

## 12.1 Types

- horizontal rule;
- vertical rule;
- full-width section line;
- inset content divider;
- numbered divider;
- label embedded in line;
- dotted or dashed technical line;
- gradient fade used sparingly;
- whitespace separator;
- color-field transition.

## 12.2 Separator rules

- A divider should have a reason.
- Prefer whitespace for weak separation.
- Use lines for stronger structural separation.
- Do not add decorative lines between every paragraph.
- Align dividers to the grid.
- Define consistent inset behavior.
- Ensure line contrast is visible without dominating.
- Do not use a separator as the only indication of a semantic section when headings are required.

## 12.3 Signature separator

A corporate identity may define one signature treatment:

```text
Section index + line
Short brand-color bar
Offset double rule
Vertical line extending into media
Label crossing a horizontal rule
```

Use it selectively to create recognition.

---

## 13. Arrows, connectors, and process lines

Use connectors for real relationships.

Approved:

- process sequence;
- flow direction;
- data dependency;
- timeline;
- organizational relationship;
- geographic route.

Rules:

- Arrow direction must be unambiguous.
- Avoid crossing lines.
- Provide nonvisual structure in HTML.
- Do not rely only on animation.
- Use consistent heads, weights, and corners.
- Recompose complex diagrams on mobile.
- Do not use decorative arrows that point to nothing.

---

## 14. Shapes and graphic motifs

## 14.1 Possible motifs

- rectangles;
- lines;
- corner brackets;
- cropped circles;
- grids;
- coordinates;
- technical marks;
- bars;
- data ticks;
- custom brand geometry;
- image windows;
- oversized letterforms;
- modular blocks.

## 14.2 Rules

- Select one primary geometry family.
- Derive motifs from brand or subject where possible.
- Use shapes to organize, frame, point, or emphasize.
- Avoid random blobs.
- Avoid decorative geometry that resembles functional UI.
- Keep background forms away from small text.
- Maintain consistent edge and radius language.

---

## 15. Patterns and textures

Expressive minimalism usually uses low-intensity texture.

Possible:

- fine grid;
- dot matrix;
- subtle grain;
- line pattern;
- map contour;
- material macro texture;
- technical hatch;
- brand-derived repeat.

Rules:

- Texture should be nearly invisible at first glance.
- Avoid trendy grain over every surface.
- Do not reduce text contrast.
- Use CSS or SVG for simple patterns.
- Compress raster textures.
- Avoid visible repetition seams.
- Define where texture is allowed.
- Remove nonessential texture on small screens if it adds noise.

---

## 16. Charts and data visualization

Data must be accurate before it is decorative.

## 16.1 Chart selection

- Bar: comparison
- Line: change over time
- Area: cumulative magnitude with care
- Scatter: relationship
- Donut or pie: limited part-to-whole categories
- Table: precise values
- Map: geographic distribution
- Timeline: sequence over time
- KPI: one contextual metric

## 16.2 Chart rules

- Use the simplest chart that communicates the point.
- Start axes honestly.
- Include units and time range.
- Label directly where possible.
- Avoid 3D effects.
- Avoid unnecessary grid lines.
- Do not use brand color for every series.
- Preserve color-blind distinguishability.
- Use patterns, labels, or symbols when color is insufficient.
- Provide a table or text equivalent for important data.
- Do not animate charts before users can read them.
- Never invent sample data in production designs without marking it clearly.

## 16.3 Data style

Define:

- axis typography;
- label style;
- grid line weight;
- series palette;
- highlight color;
- tooltip structure;
- annotation style;
- empty and error states.

---

## 17. Diagrams and process graphics

## 17.1 Common types

- step process;
- ecosystem map;
- architecture diagram;
- service model;
- lifecycle;
- funnel;
- responsibility model;
- timeline;
- system flow;
- comparison;
- before and after.

## 17.2 Diagram rules

- Give each diagram one message.
- Limit node types.
- Use consistent shapes for consistent meanings.
- Use real labels.
- Include a legend only when needed.
- Keep reading direction predictable.
- Avoid decorative complexity.
- Provide a responsive alternative.
- Preserve semantic text outside SVG where practical.
- Ensure downloadable diagrams remain readable.

---

## 18. Maps

Use maps only when geography matters.

Suitable for:

- offices;
- service coverage;
- routes;
- projects;
- infrastructure;
- markets;
- logistics networks.

Rules:

- Highlight relevant geography only.
- Avoid overly detailed basemaps.
- Include labels where users need them.
- Do not rely on hover alone.
- Provide a textual location list.
- Use accessible controls for interactive maps.
- Avoid decorative world maps that merely imply “global.”

---

## 19. Logos, customer marks, and certifications

## 19.1 Customer logos

- Use approved assets.
- Preserve proportions.
- Provide equal visual weight.
- Do not recolor without permission.
- Avoid tiny unreadable logo walls.
- Add context such as “Selected customers.”
- Use grayscale only if permitted.
- Do not imply endorsement beyond the actual relationship.

## 19.2 Certification marks

- Follow owner guidelines.
- Do not redraw.
- Keep required clear space.
- Link to evidence where appropriate.
- Do not use expired or irrelevant marks.
- Explain significance if users may not recognize it.

## 19.3 Responsive logo systems

Prepare:

- full horizontal mark;
- compact mark;
- symbol;
- light version;
- dark version;
- monochrome version;
- small-size optimized version.

Do not solve small-size problems by shrinking a complex full logo indefinitely.

---

## 20. Badges, labels, and tags

Use for:

- category;
- status;
- certification;
- date;
- region;
- content type;
- availability;
- new or updated state.

Rules:

- Keep labels short.
- Do not turn every metadata item into a pill.
- Use shape and color consistently.
- Do not make decorative badges look clickable.
- Use semantic status colors carefully.
- Ensure text remains readable.
- Avoid “NEW” and “AI” badges without meaningful context.

---

## 21. Interface screenshots

For software and digital-service companies:

- show real interface;
- focus on one workflow;
- crop to relevant area;
- keep text legible;
- avoid fictional floating dashboards;
- disclose conceptual UI;
- remove sensitive data;
- use realistic data;
- show responsive context when relevant;
- do not distort perspective so strongly that the product cannot be evaluated.

Possible framing:

- clean browser window;
- device frame used sparingly;
- full-bleed crop;
- annotated detail;
- step sequence;
- before/after comparison.

---

## 22. Video and motion assets

Use video when movement communicates something photography cannot.

Suitable:

- real operation;
- product behavior;
- location and scale;
- process;
- human story;
- controlled brand film.

Rules:

- Do not autoplay audio.
- Provide pause control where required.
- Supply captions.
- Avoid long decorative loops.
- Use a meaningful poster image.
- Optimize multiple resolutions.
- Do not place essential messaging only in video.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Avoid full-screen video when it delays core content.

---

## 23. Accessibility

## 23.1 Alternative text

### Informative

Describe the useful content or purpose.

### Functional

Describe the action.

### Decorative

Use empty alt text or appropriate programmatic hiding.

### Complex

Provide a concise alt plus nearby detailed explanation or structured equivalent.

## 23.2 Text over imagery

- Use a solid panel or reliable scrim.
- Test all responsive crops.
- Maintain text contrast.
- Do not assume the image will always have a dark region.
- Keep focus indicators visible.
- Avoid body text over moving video.

## 23.3 Non-text contrast

Meaningful component boundaries, states, icons, and graphical objects must remain perceivable.

## 23.4 Motion

- Support reduced motion.
- Do not use flashing effects.
- Allow pause where necessary.
- Preserve content without animation.

## 24. Responsive asset delivery

Use responsive formats and art direction.

Example:

```html
<picture>
  <source
    media="(max-width: 47.99rem)"
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/media/hero-mobile-640.avif 640w,
            /media/hero-mobile-960.avif 960w"
    sizes="100vw"
  />
  <source
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/media/hero-960.avif 960w,
            /media/hero-1440.avif 1440w,
            /media/hero-1920.avif 1920w"
    sizes="(min-width: 90rem) 90rem, 100vw"
  />
  <img
    src="/media/hero-1440.webp"
    width="1920"
    height="1080"
    alt="Specific alternative text"
    decoding="async"
    fetchpriority="high"
  />
</picture>
```

Rules:

- Use `srcset` and `sizes`.
- Specify intrinsic dimensions.
- Prioritize the LCP image.
- Lazy-load below-fold media.
- Use separate mobile crops where required.
- Do not ship source-resolution assets.
- Test image quality after compression.
- Use SVG for suitable graphics.
- Avoid large icon libraries when only a small subset is needed.

## 25. File formats

### AVIF

Use for:

- photography;
- complex backgrounds;
- high compression efficiency.

### WebP

Use for:

- broad modern support;
- photography;
- transparent raster graphics.

### JPEG

Use for:

- fallback;
- photography where required.

### PNG

Use for:

- crisp transparent raster;
- special source assets;
- pixel-accurate UI captures when lossy compression is harmful.

### SVG

Use for:

- icons;
- logos;
- diagrams;
- patterns;
- separators;
- line illustration;
- simple charts.

Do not store large raster imagery inside SVG.

## 28. Anti-generic rules

Avoid:

- generic blue-purple gradient backgrounds;
- floating translucent cards unrelated to content;
- random 3D spheres;
- abstract AI mesh imagery;
- icons inside identical colored circles;
- handshakes;
- pointing at blank glass walls;
- fake dashboard composites;
- city skylines used for every industry;
- decorative world maps;
- thin gray borders with insufficient contrast;
- excessive pills;
- meaningless dotted lines;
- abstract waves between every section;
- mixed icon packs;
- unlicensed customer logos;
- charts with fake data;
- background video with no informational purpose;
- one image filter applied to every asset regardless of role.

## 29. AI image-generation instructions

A prompt should specify:

1. visual direction;
2. sector;
3. actual activity;
4. environment;
5. people and roles;
6. lighting;
7. composition;
8. camera perspective;
9. palette;
10. intended page role;
11. negative-space requirement;
12. aspect ratio;
13. modern details to include or exclude;
14. realism level;
15. items that must not appear.

Example:

```text
Documentary corporate photograph for a Nordic maritime logistics company.
Realistic warehouse operation beside a port terminal, one warehouse coordinator
checking labeled cargo while a forklift operates safely in the background.
Natural overcast daylight, accurate industrial colors, confident but unstaged mood.
Wide editorial composition with clear negative space on the left for an HTML headline.
Authentic PPE and equipment, no fake holograms, no visible brand logos, no text,
no handshake, no posed stock-photo smiles. 16:9.
```

Generated visuals must not be presented as documentary evidence, actual employees, real facilities, or completed client work.

## 30. AI-agent instructions

An AI agent building the visual system must:

1. identify which assets provide evidence;
2. choose one primary visual direction;
3. define role-specific image treatments;
4. define icon grid and stroke;
5. define border and separator rules;
6. define chart and diagram rules;
7. define responsive image ratios;
8. define alt-text strategy;
9. ensure rights and provenance are recorded;
10. avoid fake proof;
11. optimize all exports;
12. preserve useful content without visual effects;
13. separate decorative and informative assets;
14. document all exceptions.

The agent must not:

- invent customer logos;
- invent project photography;
- generate false documentary evidence;
- mix unrelated icon styles;
- use graphics without purpose;
- create charts from fabricated data;
- place critical text inside images;
- use inaccessible boundaries;
- export only one oversized raster file;
- infer licenses.

## 33. Final rule

A modern corporate visual system should make the organization more understandable and credible. Photography supplies evidence, diagrams explain, icons guide, borders organize, separators establish rhythm, and decorative graphics create identity. When these roles are confused, the site becomes generic or noisy; when they are coordinated, minimal design becomes distinctive.
