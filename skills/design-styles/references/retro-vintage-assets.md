# Retro & Vintage Website Image Style Guide

> A general-purpose image and art-direction reference for retro- and vintage-inspired websites.
>
> This document covers photography, illustration, collage, texture, image treatment, cropping, responsive delivery, accessibility, and production. It is brand-neutral.

---

## 1. Purpose

Images carry much of the historical character in a retro or vintage website. Their style should be selected deliberately and applied consistently across photography, illustration, textures, thumbnails, banners, backgrounds, and social images.

The goal is not to make every image look old. The goal is to establish a coherent visual world associated with a selected era while preserving:

- subject clarity;
- visual hierarchy;
- accessible text contrast;
- responsive cropping;
- fast loading;
- authentic art direction.

---

## 2. Select the image era

Before generating, sourcing, or editing images, define the primary visual period.

| Era | Photography and image characteristics |
|---|---|
| Victorian / archival | Engravings, botanical plates, formal portraiture, monochrome or sepia, ornate frames |
| Art Nouveau | Organic illustration, botanical curves, poster art, decorative figure composition |
| Art Deco | Stylized luxury, symmetry, dramatic lighting, geometric framing, polished products |
| 1930s–1950s print | Black-and-white documentary photography, hand-tinted images, diagrams, catalog cuts |
| Mid-century | Flat illustration, bold crop, optimistic lifestyle imagery, geometric collage |
| 1950s advertising | Highly staged product scenes, painted illustration, saturated spot colors, clean smiles |
| 1960s | Fashion editorial, pop-art color, optical pattern, high contrast, experimental crop |
| 1970s | Warm film cast, natural interiors, flash photography, soft focus, earthy palette |
| 1980s | Direct flash, neon, chrome, studio gradients, airbrush, VHS and CRT references |
| 1990s | Snapshot photography, disposable-camera flash, photocopy, grunge collage, oversaturated print |
| Early digital | Pixel art, low-resolution UI graphics, dither, primitive 3D, scanner artifacts |

Do not apply the same image treatment to every era.

---

## 3. Image-system principles

### 3.1 Art direction before filters

A convincing retro image depends more on:

- subject;
- wardrobe;
- environment;
- props;
- lighting;
- camera angle;
- composition;
- print treatment;

than on a color filter.

A modern office photo with a sepia overlay rarely becomes authentic vintage imagery.

### 3.2 One dominant production method

Select one dominant image language:

- film photography;
- studio advertising;
- editorial documentary;
- screen print;
- halftone print;
- engraving;
- photocopy;
- collage;
- pixel art;
- airbrush;
- technical illustration.

Secondary treatments may support it, but should not compete.

### 3.3 Controlled aging

Aging should reflect a plausible material process:

- paper yellowing;
- faded ink;
- film grain;
- dust;
- scratches;
- fold marks;
- ink spread;
- imperfect registration;
- photocopy contrast;
- print dots.

Do not add every damage effect at once.

### 3.4 Preserve subject recognition

Distress should never make the primary subject, product, person, or action difficult to understand.

### 3.5 Image treatment must serve hierarchy

Hero images may carry stronger styling. Functional product images, documentation, diagrams, and UI screenshots usually require more clarity and less distress.

## 4. Photography styles

## 4.1 Archival monochrome

Characteristics:

- grayscale or warm monochrome;
- moderate contrast;
- visible film grain;
- soft edge falloff;
- subtle dust;
- restrained vignetting;
- documentary or formal composition.

Use for:

- history;
- institutions;
- biographies;
- cultural projects;
- editorial storytelling.

Avoid artificial heavy scratches on every image.

## 4.2 Mid-century editorial photography

Characteristics:

- bold geometric composition;
- clean negative space;
- strong product or subject silhouette;
- limited color relationships;
- controlled studio light;
- graphic crops.

Use for:

- design studios;
- architecture;
- culture;
- products;
- hospitality.

## 4.3 1950s commercial advertising

Characteristics:

- idealized scenes;
- highly controlled lighting;
- saturated but limited palette;
- polished products;
- optimistic expression;
- painted or retouched finish;
- headline-friendly negative space.

Use with awareness that historical advertising often reflected outdated social stereotypes. Recreate the visual language, not harmful representation.

## 4.4 1960s fashion and pop

Characteristics:

- dramatic crop;
- strong makeup or styling;
- bold flat backgrounds;
- high contrast;
- color blocking;
- fisheye or unusual angle;
- poster-like repetition.

## 4.5 1970s film

Characteristics:

- warm color cast;
- slightly lowered contrast;
- soft highlight roll-off;
- visible but fine grain;
- natural light;
- brown, gold, green, cream, and rust;
- candid or lifestyle framing.

Avoid making skin tones unnaturally orange.

## 4.6 1980s studio

Characteristics:

- direct flash or hard studio light;
- colored gels;
- glossy surfaces;
- gradient backdrops;
- chrome and glass;
- dramatic rim light;
- airbrushed retouching;
- saturated neon accents.

Use glow around lights, not around all edges and text.

## 4.7 1990s snapshot

Characteristics:

- direct on-camera flash;
- imperfect framing;
- casual posture;
- slight red-eye or highlight clipping where appropriate;
- consumer-film color;
- date-stamp option;
- candid energy.

The image should feel spontaneous, not simply low quality.

---

## 5. Illustration styles

## 5.1 Engraving and etching

Characteristics:

- cross-hatching;
- fine line density;
- monochrome or limited ink;
- high detail;
- historical scientific or editorial character.

Rules:

- maintain enough resolution for line detail;
- simplify at small sizes;
- do not pair detailed engraving with ultra-minimal icons;
- use real vector or high-resolution raster sources.

## 5.2 Letterpress and woodcut

Characteristics:

- bold carved shapes;
- rough edges;
- ink variation;
- limited colors;
- strong silhouettes.

Useful for:

- badges;
- editorial features;
- food and beverage;
- craft;
- cultural events.

## 5.3 Mid-century flat illustration

Characteristics:

- simplified anatomy and objects;
- geometric forms;
- limited spot colors;
- minimal shading;
- intentional negative space;
- playful scale.

## 5.4 Retro cartoon

Characteristics:

- thick outlines;
- rubber-hose or period-specific character design;
- expressive poses;
- flat fills;
- halftone shading;
- limited palette.

Avoid mixing animation eras without intent.

## 5.5 Technical and catalog illustration

Characteristics:

- exploded views;
- cutaways;
- numbered parts;
- instruction arrows;
- monochrome line art;
- utility labels.

Maintain functional accuracy. Decorative aging must not obscure technical meaning.

## 5.6 Airbrush and chrome illustration

Characteristics:

- smooth gradient;
- reflected highlights;
- chrome surfaces;
- dramatic horizon;
- space-age or 1980s commercial finish.

Use selectively because large areas of synthetic gloss can dominate the interface.

## 5.7 Pixel art

Characteristics:

- intentional low-resolution grid;
- fixed palette;
- hard edges;
- no accidental smoothing;
- nearest-neighbor scaling.

Rules:

- scale by integer multiples where possible;
- do not use blurry interpolation;
- preserve readable silhouettes;
- use modern accessible labels around pixel icons.

---

## 6. Collage styles

## 6.1 Editorial paper collage

Elements:

- paper cutouts;
- photographs;
- printed shapes;
- tape;
- labels;
- handwriting;
- torn edges.

Maintain a controlled depth system:

```text
Background paper
Large image layer
Graphic shape
Text label
Small annotation
Texture overlay
```

## 6.2 Photomontage

Use scale contrast, hard masks, and unexpected juxtaposition. Strongest for:

- editorial hero sections;
- music;
- culture;
- campaigns;
- conceptual storytelling.

Do not use complex montage behind dense body copy.

## 6.3 Zine and photocopy collage

Characteristics:

- high-contrast black and white;
- rough cut edges;
- enlarged halftone;
- scanner shadows;
- handwritten notes;
- staple or tape references;
- deliberate misalignment.

The layout should still have a semantic reading order.

## 6.4 Scrapbook

Characteristics:

- layered keepsakes;
- postcards;
- receipts;
- tickets;
- stamps;
- photo corners;
- annotations.

Use repeated object types and a consistent paper family. Random stock assets create an incoherent result.

---

## 7. Color treatment

## 7.1 Faded print

Methods:

- slightly compressed tonal range;
- lifted blacks;
- muted highlights;
- reduced saturation;
- subtle paper color influence;
- mild color shift.

Avoid reducing contrast so far that the focal subject disappears.

## 7.2 Limited spot color

Convert images into:

- monochrome + one accent;
- duotone;
- tritone;
- black ink + two spot colors.

This is effective for mid-century, screen-print, newspaper, punk, and catalog styles.

## 7.3 Sepia

Use for true archival or historical direction, not as a universal vintage filter.

Better approach:

- warm grayscale;
- restrained brown tint;
- preserved neutral shadows;
- moderate contrast.

## 7.4 Cross-processed or consumer film

Possible effects:

- green or cyan shadows;
- warm highlights;
- shifted skin tones;
- saturated reds;
- compressed dynamic range.

Use consistently and test across varied subject matter.

## 7.5 Neon and chromatic glow

For 1980s or retro-futurist images:

- keep the base image dark;
- use one or two glow colors;
- preserve dark negative space;
- avoid glow over faces and essential details;
- retain a clear focal point.

---

## 8. Grain, halftone, and print effects

## 8.1 Film grain

Good grain:

- scales with image size;
- varies naturally;
- is stronger in shadows or midtones where appropriate;
- does not cover text;
- remains subtle on mobile.

Bad grain:

- identical noise pattern on every image;
- oversized digital noise;
- heavy grain over small faces;
- compression artifacts mistaken for film texture.

## 8.2 Halftone

Types:

- round dots;
- line screens;
- coarse newspaper dots;
- CMYK rosette;
- single-color screen print.

Rules:

- ensure dot size remains intentional at target display size;
- use larger dots for decorative hero imagery;
- use finer dots for small cards;
- avoid moiré;
- test responsive scaling;
- consider pre-rendering rather than relying on expensive runtime filters.

## 8.3 Registration offset

Use a slight offset between color plates.

Recommended:

- one or two channels only;
- small displacement;
- strongest on edges;
- not applied to live text;
- reduced for small assets.

## 8.4 Ink bleed and spread

Appropriate for:

- letterpress;
- newspaper;
- screen print;
- stamps.

The effect should soften or expand printed forms slightly. Do not reduce icon or label recognition.

## 8.5 Photocopy

Characteristics:

- crushed black;
- blown highlights;
- toner noise;
- edge shadow;
- repeated-generation artifacts;
- streaks and dust.

Use for 1980s–1990s punk, zine, and underground editorial styles.

---

## 9. Texture library

A coherent project should use a small approved texture set.

Recommended categories:

- paper grain;
- fold or crease;
- print noise;
- dust;
- scratch;
- tape;
- ink stamp;
- halftone;
- scanner edge;
- fabric or canvas.

### Texture rules

- one primary paper texture;
- one print texture;
- one optional damage texture;
- no more than two visible textures in a small component;
- lower texture opacity beneath text;
- avoid repeating the exact damage pattern prominently;
- use masks and varied positioning;
- optimize and compress assets;
- do not use giant full-resolution scans when a small seamless texture is sufficient.

---

## 10. Framing and masks

Possible image frames:

- thin printed rule;
- double-line Art Deco frame;
- rounded slide mount;
- polaroid;
- film strip;
- postage stamp;
- ticket edge;
- torn paper;
- oval portrait;
- archival card;
- CRT screen;
- beveled software window.

Select one primary frame type and one secondary exception.

### Masking rules

- cropping must preserve the focal subject;
- decorative shapes should not clip essential faces or products;
- avoid applying a different mask to every card;
- mobile may require a separate crop rather than scaling the desktop mask;
- provide an unmasked version for contexts requiring clarity.

---

## 11. Composition and cropping

## 11.1 Hero images

A hero image should include intentional negative space for:

- title;
- supporting copy;
- primary action;
- labels or metadata.

Do not place text over visually busy regions without a supporting surface or contrast treatment.

## 11.2 Editorial crops

Useful techniques:

- extreme close crop;
- subject crossing the grid;
- image bleeding to edge;
- portrait aligned against a rule;
- image spanning multiple columns;
- caption connected by line or number.

## 11.3 Product images

Retro treatment must not misrepresent:

- product color;
- condition;
- size;
- finish;
- included parts.

Keep documentary product views neutral where accuracy matters. Apply the historical style through framing, background, type, and supporting graphics.

## 11.4 Portraits

Rules:

- maintain natural skin tone unless a stylized treatment is explicit;
- do not allow grain or halftone to destroy eyes and facial features;
- use period-appropriate wardrobe, lighting, and background;
- preserve sufficient separation between subject and text;
- prepare alternate crops for desktop and mobile.

---

## 12. Image roles

Define image treatments by role.

| Role | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|
| Hero | Strongest art direction, deliberate composition, larger texture detail |
| Editorial feature | Era-consistent crop and color, moderate texture |
| Card thumbnail | Simplified treatment, consistent ratio, reduced distress |
| Product image | High clarity, restrained treatment |
| Portrait | Consistent lighting and crop, controlled grain |
| Background | Low contrast, nonessential, aggressively optimized |
| Icon or diagram | Crisp edges, minimal texture, high legibility |
| Social image | Bold type-safe composition, recognizable at small preview size |
| Logo | No raster aging unless part of the approved identity asset |

Do not process all roles with one identical preset.

---

## 13. Text inside images

Meaningful headings, labels, prices, calls to action, and navigation should normally remain HTML text.

Text may be part of an image when:

- it is an authentic photographed object;
- it is an illustration or poster where the lettering is essential;
- it is a logo;
- it is decorative and repeated in accessible HTML elsewhere.

Requirements:

- provide appropriate alternative text;
- do not place critical instructions only in an image;
- ensure the image remains legible at responsive sizes;
- avoid tiny generated lettering;
- do not rely on AI-generated text inside images for exact wording.

---

## 14. AI image-generation instructions

When generating a retro or vintage website image, the prompt should specify:

1. exact era;
2. image medium;
3. subject;
4. setting;
5. wardrobe or object styling;
6. lighting;
7. composition;
8. palette;
9. print or film characteristics;
10. intended page role;
11. required negative space;
12. aspect ratio;
13. excluded modern elements.

Example structure:

```text
1970s editorial lifestyle photograph, photographed on warm consumer color film.
Subject: two people repairing a radio in a wood-paneled workshop.
Natural side light, subtle direct-flash fill, warm cream, rust, olive, and brown.
Slight film grain, soft highlight roll-off, authentic period clothing and tools.
Wide composition with uncluttered negative space on the left for a website headline.
No modern electronics, no contemporary logos, no fake heavy scratches, no text.
Aspect ratio 16:9.
```

### Negative prompt principles

Exclude:

- modern clothing or devices;
- unrelated historical periods;
- illegible text;
- random logos;
- excessive aging;
- plastic-looking skin;
- cinematic effects inconsistent with the era;
- modern minimalist studio backgrounds where inappropriate;
- unintentional extra objects or anatomy errors.

---

## 15. Sourcing real images

When sourcing archival or stock imagery:

- verify usage rights;
- preserve creator and archive attribution where required;
- check whether modification is permitted;
- avoid presenting modern reenactments as historical fact;
- distinguish documentary images from illustrative material;
- maintain records of source, license, and credit;
- avoid culturally insensitive or misleading period imagery;
- do not remove watermarks;
- do not assume “old” means public domain.

---

## 16. Responsive image delivery

Use responsive images rather than delivering one large file to every device.

Example:

```html
<picture>
  <source
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="
      /images/feature-640.avif 640w,
      /images/feature-960.avif 960w,
      /images/feature-1440.avif 1440w
    "
    sizes="(min-width: 64rem) 50vw, 100vw"
  />
  <source
    type="image/webp"
    srcset="
      /images/feature-640.webp 640w,
      /images/feature-960.webp 960w,
      /images/feature-1440.webp 1440w
    "
    sizes="(min-width: 64rem) 50vw, 100vw"
  />
  <img
    src="/images/feature-960.jpg"
    width="1440"
    height="960"
    alt="Descriptive alternative text"
    loading="lazy"
    decoding="async"
  />
</picture>
```

### Rules

- provide widths appropriate to actual rendered sizes;
- specify intrinsic width and height;
- use art-directed crops when mobile composition differs;
- lazy-load below-the-fold images;
- do not lazy-load the primary LCP hero image by default;
- use AVIF or WebP where suitable;
- preserve PNG or SVG where transparency or crisp graphic detail requires it;
- avoid animated GIF for long animation;
- test texture and halftone compression carefully.

---

## 17. File format guidance

### AVIF

Best for:

- photographs;
- gradients;
- complex textures;
- strong compression.

Test fine grain and very small detail because aggressive compression may alter intentional texture.

### WebP

Best for:

- broad browser compatibility;
- photography;
- transparent raster graphics;
- medium-complexity texture.

### JPEG

Useful as:

- fallback;
- photography where universal support matters;
- controlled progressive loading.

### PNG

Use for:

- transparency;
- small pixel graphics;
- crisp raster assets;
- source assets where lossy compression is harmful.

Do not use PNG for large full-color photographs unless required.

### SVG

Use for:

- icons;
- line art;
- geometric illustration;
- borders;
- patterns;
- stamps;
- simple technical diagrams.

Avoid embedding huge photographic textures as base64 inside SVG.

---

## 18. Accessibility

Every meaningful image needs an alternative-text decision.

### Informative image

Describe the information or purpose, not every visual detail.

### Functional image

Describe the action or destination.

### Decorative image

Use empty alternative text:

```html
alt=""
```

### Complex diagram

Provide:

- concise alt text;
- nearby detailed explanation;
- data table or structured equivalent where relevant.

### Text contrast over images

- provide a solid or semi-opaque backing surface;
- use a gradient scrim only when it reliably preserves contrast;
- test across responsive crops;
- do not depend on one area of the photo remaining dark;
- keep focus indicators visible over imagery.

---

## 19. Performance budget

Suggested project targets should be adapted to context, but image-heavy retro sites should define a budget.

Example:

```text
Hero image: ideally below 250–400 KB at common desktop size
Card image: ideally below 50–120 KB
Texture tile: ideally below 10–40 KB
Decorative SVG: ideally below 20–60 KB
Initial page image transfer: controlled and measured
```

Rules:

- do not ship source-resolution scans;
- resize before compression;
- remove unnecessary metadata;
- use responsive variants;
- avoid stacking several full-screen textures;
- reuse optimized texture assets;
- reserve space to prevent layout shift;
- measure the final page, not just individual files.

## 20. Image consistency matrix

Create an image matrix before production.

| Property | Decision |
|---|---|
| Primary era | Define exact decade or movement |
| Medium | Film, print, engraving, collage, etc. |
| Color treatment | Full color, monochrome, duotone, spot color |
| Grain | None, fine, medium, coarse |
| Contrast | Soft, normal, high, photocopy |
| Crop | Centered, asymmetric, editorial, full bleed |
| Frame | None, rule, slide, polaroid, ticket, CRT |
| Texture | Paper, ink, dust, scanner, none |
| Aspect ratios | Hero, card, portrait, social |
| Lighting | Natural, flash, studio, dramatic |
| Negative space | Left, right, top, none |
| Mobile art direction | Separate crop or same image |
| Accessibility | Alt-text strategy and text overlay rules |

All image assets should follow this matrix unless an exception is documented.

## 21. Common failures

Avoid:

- using sepia as the only historical signal;
- mixing film grain, VHS noise, paper tears, and engraving in one image;
- applying identical distress to every asset;
- placing small text over busy photography;
- using modern props in a historical scene;
- using fake aging that obscures the subject;
- inconsistent grain scale;
- accidental low-resolution imagery;
- blurry pixel art;
- modern cinematic teal-orange grading for every era;
- overusing polaroid frames;
- relying on AI-generated text inside images;
- misrepresenting real products through stylized treatment;
- serving desktop hero files unchanged to mobile;
- using archival imagery without license verification;
- treating documentary images as decoration without context.

---

## 22. AI-agent production rules

An AI agent preparing images for a retro or vintage website must:

1. identify the primary era;
2. select one dominant image medium;
3. define role-specific treatments;
4. maintain a consistent palette and grain scale;
5. prepare desktop and mobile crops;
6. preserve subject recognition;
7. avoid critical text inside raster assets;
8. generate descriptive file names;
9. produce modern optimized formats;
10. include intrinsic dimensions;
11. create alt-text recommendations;
12. document license or generation source;
13. verify that image treatment does not reduce contrast or usability;
14. avoid unrelated modern or historical details.

The agent must not:

- apply arbitrary “vintage filters” without art direction;
- use the same preset for hero, product, and diagram images;
- embed important interface text in images;
- add random brand marks;
- generate inaccurate historical claims;
- use excessive distress;
- export only one oversized image;
- ignore mobile cropping;
- invent licenses or archive provenance.

## 23. File naming

Use descriptive, predictable names.

```text
hero-1970s-workshop-wide.avif
hero-1970s-workshop-mobile.avif
portrait-editorial-warm-01.webp
catalog-radio-front-neutral.webp
texture-paper-cream-light.webp
texture-halftone-round-small.svg
illustration-midcentury-tools-01.svg
```

Avoid:

```text
final.jpg
vintage2-new-final.png
image123.webp
```

## 24. Production checklist

### Direction

- [ ] Era selected
- [ ] Medium selected
- [ ] Reference set approved
- [ ] Modern elements to exclude listed
- [ ] Image role defined

### Art direction

- [ ] Subject and setting fit the era
- [ ] Lighting is consistent
- [ ] Wardrobe and props are credible
- [ ] Palette is consistent
- [ ] Negative space supports layout
- [ ] Crop works with text placement

### Treatment

- [ ] Grain scale is consistent
- [ ] Aging is restrained
- [ ] Halftone does not produce moiré
- [ ] Registration offset is controlled
- [ ] Subject remains recognizable
- [ ] Skin tones remain intentional
- [ ] Texture does not obscure detail

### Responsive delivery

- [ ] Desktop and mobile crops prepared
- [ ] Correct aspect ratios exported
- [ ] Responsive widths generated
- [ ] Width and height attributes included
- [ ] Hero loading strategy correct
- [ ] Below-fold images lazy-loaded

### Accessibility

- [ ] Alt text written or marked decorative
- [ ] Text overlay contrast tested
- [ ] Critical information exists outside image
- [ ] Diagrams have structured explanation
- [ ] Functional images have action-focused labels

### Performance and rights

- [ ] Modern formats exported
- [ ] File sizes checked
- [ ] Metadata removed where appropriate
- [ ] License documented
- [ ] Attribution retained where required
- [ ] Source or generation record stored

## 26. Final rule

A convincing retro or vintage image system is created through period-specific art direction, not a generic filter. Images should feel as though they belong to the selected era while being delivered with modern clarity, accessibility, responsive behavior, and performance.
