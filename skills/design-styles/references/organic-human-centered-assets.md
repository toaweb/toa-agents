# Organic / Human-Centered Images & Graphic Assets Guide 2026

> A comprehensive, brand-neutral guide for photography, illustration, icons, borders, separators, shapes, textures, diagrams, motion assets, representation, consent, and responsive visual production.
>
> Use this document together with `organic-human-centered-web-design-system-2026.md`.

---

## 1. Purpose

Visual assets in Organic / Human-Centered design should help people feel:

- represented;
- respected;
- informed;
- welcomed;
- oriented;
- emotionally safe;
- connected to real context.

They should not use people, culture, disability, community, or nature as decoration.

Every visual asset should have a defined role:

```text
Evidence
Explanation
Navigation
Atmosphere
```

Priority:

```text
Evidence > explanation > navigation > atmosphere
```

---

## 2. Visual direction

Suitable directions include:

- documentary human;
- community editorial;
- natural environmental;
- handmade illustration;
- local cultural;
- tactile collage;
- soft geometric;
- accessible informational;
- low-stimulation calm;
- playful inclusive.

Select one primary direction and one supporting direction.

Example:

```text
Primary: Documentary community photography
Supporting: Hand-drawn informational illustration
Avoid: Generic diversity stock, glossy 3D, random organic blobs
```

---

## 3. Representation principles

People must be represented with:

- agency;
- competence;
- context;
- dignity;
- individuality;
- consent.

Avoid:

- pity;
- tokenism;
- staged vulnerability;
- stereotypical roles;
- people reduced to diagnosis;
- anonymous “beneficiaries”;
- emotional manipulation;
- false community representation.

Do not present AI-generated people as real participants, staff, users, patients, students, or community members.

---

## 4. Photography strategy

Create image systems rather than isolated stock selections.

A complete story may include:

1. place;
2. activity;
3. person in context;
4. interaction;
5. detail;
6. environment;
7. outcome;
8. portrait;
9. group or community;
10. support or service.

Photography should help users understand what the organization, place, or service is actually like.

---

## 5. Documentary photography

Characteristics:

- real location;
- real activity;
- credible interaction;
- natural behavior;
- contextual detail;
- honest emotion;
- accurate captioning.

Do not direct people into performative happiness or distress.

Documentary truth must take priority over visual perfection.

---

## 6. Portraiture

Portraits may use:

- natural environment;
- direct or relaxed gaze;
- soft daylight;
- role-relevant context;
- consistent crop;
- personal but respectful styling.

Define:

```text
Camera height
Lens
Background
Lighting
Expression
Wardrobe
Crop
Aspect ratio
Color treatment
Consent
Caption
```

Avoid photographing every person in exactly the same generic corporate pose if individuality matters.

---

## 7. Community and group imagery

Show:

- participation;
- collaboration;
- conversation;
- learning;
- making;
- shared activity;
- public space;
- support;
- celebration.

Do not arrange groups solely to display demographic variety.

Group composition should reflect real context.

---

## 8. Disability representation

Represent disability through:

- real participation;
- accessible environments;
- adaptive tools;
- expertise;
- daily context;
- independence;
- interdependence;
- choice.

Avoid:

- inspiration stereotypes;
- helpless framing;
- medical close-ups without context;
- accessibility equipment used as props;
- cropping mobility aids out of the image;
- treating accessibility as tragedy or heroism.

Consult subjects and relevant communities.

---

## 9. Children and vulnerable people

Use additional safeguards:

- informed consent;
- guardian consent where required;
- privacy;
- appropriate naming;
- location protection;
- image lifespan;
- context;
- future reuse;
- withdrawal process.

Do not use emotionally sensitive imagery merely to increase donations or engagement.

---

## 10. Natural and environmental imagery

Use nature where it is relevant to:

- place;
- activity;
- ecosystem;
- material;
- season;
- community;
- wellbeing;
- sustainability.

Avoid generic leaves, forests, hands holding soil, or landscapes used to imply unsupported environmental claims.

Caption species, location, process, or impact where relevant.

---

## 11. Photography composition

Suitable composition includes:

- human-scale context;
- eye-level perspective;
- quiet asymmetry;
- natural movement;
- environmental detail;
- open negative space;
- layered relationships;
- non-central subjects.

Avoid surveillance-like high angles or dehumanizing distance unless required for context.

---

## 12. Color treatment

Preferred treatments:

### Natural documentary

Accurate skin, environment, and material color.

### Warm editorial

Subtle warmth, restrained saturation, and visible tonal detail.

### Local color

Color treatment informed by place, climate, culture, and material.

### Monochrome

Use selectively for archive, portrait, or visual consistency.

Do not use a universal earthy filter that erases cultural and environmental color differences.

---

## 13. Retouching

Retouching should not erase:

- age;
- disability;
- skin texture;
- body shape;
- assistive device;
- environmental reality;
- cultural marker.

Allowed adjustments include exposure, white balance, crop, dust, and temporary distraction.

Do not alter people to fit a visual ideal.

---

## 14. Captions and stories

Captions may include:

- name;
- role;
- activity;
- place;
- date;
- photographer;
- consent restrictions;
- context.

Use names only when consent allows.

Avoid labels that define a person only by disability, diagnosis, social status, or need.

---

## 15. Alternative text

Alternative text should communicate the useful content and context.

Good:

```text
Mina uses a tactile map with a museum guide during a public workshop.
```

Avoid:

```text
Disabled woman at event.
```

The first version describes activity and context rather than reducing the person to an attribute.

Complex graphics require detailed descriptions.

Decorative assets use empty alt text.

---

## 16. Illustration

Suitable directions:

- hand-drawn line;
- painted editorial;
- cut-paper;
- printmaking;
- soft geometric;
- collage;
- character illustration;
- local visual tradition;
- botanical or environmental study;
- accessible informational drawing.

Define:

```text
Stroke
Fill
Palette
Edge
Perspective
Human proportions
Facial detail
Texture
Mobility and assistive-device treatment
Cultural references
```

---

## 17. Inclusive illustration

Illustrated people should reflect variation in:

- age;
- body;
- mobility;
- role;
- gender expression;
- skin tone;
- clothing;
- family structure;
- work;
- environment.

Avoid treating inclusion as a checklist of visible traits.

Characters should have activity, emotion, and context.

Do not make assistive devices inaccurate or decorative.

---

## 18. Mascots and characters

Mascots may create warmth and guidance.

Use them for:

- onboarding;
- help;
- feedback;
- education;
- community identity.

Do not use childish mascots in serious, high-stakes, or sensitive contexts unless research supports it.

Characters must not conceal system errors or manipulate emotional response.

---

## 19. Hand-drawn marks

Suitable marks:

- arrows;
- circles;
- underlines;
- stars;
- leaves;
- paths;
- dividers;
- annotations.

Define a repeatable stroke vocabulary.

Do not add random doodles to generic components.

Functional indicators must remain precise and accessible.

---

## 20. Icons

Icons should prioritize recognition.

Define:

```text
Grid: 20, 24, or 32
Stroke
Caps
Joins
Fill
Corner treatment
Optical size
Label rules
```

Possible style:

- softened geometric;
- humanist line;
- slightly irregular outline;
- simple filled pictogram.

Use labels for unfamiliar actions.

Avoid mixing hand-drawn icons with precise UI icons without a documented division.

---

## 21. Accessibility symbols

Use recognized accessibility symbols accurately.

Do not:

- redesign official symbols into unrecognizable forms;
- use disability symbols as decoration;
- represent all accessibility with a wheelchair symbol;
- imply complete accessibility from one icon.

Explain available features in text.

---

## 22. Borders and frames

Suitable treatments:

- warm neutral line;
- hand-drawn frame;
- soft rectangle;
- partial organic edge;
- paper cut;
- simple dark rule;
- local pattern edge.

Use one primary system.

Borders must not make controls ambiguous.

---

## 23. Separators

Suitable separators:

- whitespace;
- soft line;
- hand-drawn path;
- color transition;
- small local motif;
- organic edge;
- typographic marker.

Use separators to clarify sections and rhythm.

Do not decorate every transition.

Keep semantic section headings independent of the graphic separator.

---

## 24. Shapes and masks

Suitable shapes:

- arch;
- oval;
- soft rectangle;
- irregular paper cut;
- curved field;
- local natural form;
- hand-painted shape.

Rules:

- preserve faces and assistive devices;
- use few shape families;
- simplify on mobile;
- avoid high-frequency edges;
- do not make controls look like decoration;
- preserve source order.

---

## 25. Texture

Suitable sources:

- paper;
- fabric;
- paint;
- clay;
- print;
- recycled material;
- wood;
- local craft;
- analogue photography.

Texture should support authorship and place.

Avoid heavy texture behind text or controls.

Provide lower-stimulation and high-contrast behavior where needed.

---

## 26. Collage

Collage can combine:

- photography;
- archive;
- drawing;
- paper;
- handwritten notes;
- local material;
- maps;
- quotations.

Define a clear layer order:

```text
Base
Primary evidence
Supporting context
Annotation
Caption
Texture
```

Do not create visual clutter that harms reading or cognitive accessibility.

---

## 27. Maps and local graphics

Maps may show:

- service location;
- community resource;
- route;
- event;
- accessibility feature;
- local context.

Provide:

- clear labels;
- address list;
- transport information;
- accessible route details;
- non-map alternative.

Do not use maps only as decorative local identity.

---

## 28. Diagrams and process graphics

Use diagrams to explain:

- service journey;
- application;
- support process;
- decision;
- rights;
- eligibility;
- timeline;
- impact.

Use direct labels, limited shapes, predictable direction, sufficient contrast, and an accessible text equivalent.

Avoid overly playful diagrams for serious processes.

---

## 29. Data visualization

Human-Centered data visualization should show:

- who or what is represented;
- source;
- date;
- uncertainty;
- missing data;
- impact;
- context.

Avoid dehumanizing people into unsupported percentages.

Use plain-language summaries and tables.

Do not use red/green alone.

---

## 30. Video and audio

Use for:

- real stories;
- process;
- place;
- language access;
- sign language;
- interviews;
- instruction;
- community events.

Requirements:

- captions;
- transcript;
- audio description where relevant;
- no autoplay audio;
- pause;
- playback controls;
- sign-language version where appropriate;
- poster image;
- content summary.

Do not use emotional music to manipulate sensitive stories.

---

## 31. Motion assets

Suitable motion:

- gentle drawing;
- progress;
- guided sequence;
- small character response;
- transition;
- attention cue.

Provide:

- reduced-motion version;
- pause for repeating motion;
- no flashing;
- no essential meaning only in motion;
- stable controls;
- predictable triggers.

---

## 32. AI-generated imagery

AI may be used for:

- clearly illustrative concepts;
- abstract textures;
- non-documentary supporting art;
- early ideation.

Do not use generated people as:

- staff;
- users;
- patients;
- students;
- beneficiaries;
- community members;
- research participants.

Generated imagery may reproduce stereotypes or inaccessible environments. Review with domain and community knowledge.

---

## 33. Image generation template

```text
Human-centered editorial illustration or clearly non-documentary image.
Subject: [specific person, activity, place, or service].
Context: [credible environment and task].
Representation: dignified, active, non-stereotypical, inclusive.
Accessibility details: [accurate relevant features or devices].
Composition: human scale, clear negative space on [side].
Color: warm, distinct, sufficient contrast.
Page role: [hero, story, service, educational].
Exclude: pity framing, generic diversity stock, fake logos, illegible text,
inaccurate assistive devices, plastic skin, exaggerated emotion.
Aspect ratio: [ratio].
```

Do not claim the result depicts a real person or community.

## 34. Responsive image delivery

```html
<picture>
  <source
    media="(max-width: 48rem)"
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/images/community-mobile-640.avif 640w,
            /images/community-mobile-960.avif 960w"
    sizes="100vw"
  />
  <source
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/images/community-960.avif 960w,
            /images/community-1440.avif 1440w"
    sizes="(min-width: 86rem) 86rem, 100vw"
  />
  <img
    src="/images/community-1440.webp"
    width="1440"
    height="960"
    alt="Specific alternative text"
    loading="lazy"
    decoding="async"
  />
</picture>
```

Use mobile-specific crops, intrinsic dimensions, LCP priority, and lazy loading below the fold.


Avoid labels that expose sensitive information unnecessarily.

## 36. Rights, consent, and withdrawal

Store:

- creator;
- subject;
- date;
- location;
- consent;
- guardian consent;
- allowed channels;
- duration;
- naming permission;
- sensitive context;
- withdrawal contact;
- license;
- edits;
- AI status.

Consent should be understandable and revocable where applicable.

Do not assume past consent covers every future campaign or AI use.

---

## 37. Anti-patterns

Avoid:

- pity imagery;
- staged diversity;
- generic smiling groups;
- AI people presented as real;
- inaccurate assistive devices;
- people defined only by diagnosis;
- nature used for unsupported impact claims;
- random organic blobs;
- low-contrast earth tones;
- infantilizing illustration;
- mixed icon systems;
- texture obscuring content;
- emotional manipulation;
- inaccessible charts;
- captions without consent;
- symbolic representation replacing actual accessibility information.

---

## 38. AI-agent instructions

The agent must:

1. define representation goals;
2. identify consent and sensitivity;
3. classify evidence versus illustration;
4. plan image stories rather than isolated stock;
5. preserve agency and context;
6. define icon, border, separator, and shape systems;
7. represent assistive devices accurately;
8. define accessible alternatives;
9. record rights and withdrawal;
10. label AI-generated assets;
11. avoid stereotypes and pity framing;
12. prepare responsive crops;
13. review with relevant people where possible;
14. ensure atmosphere does not replace evidence.

## 41. Final rule

Organic / Human-Centered visual assets should represent people with dignity, accuracy, agency, and context. Photography provides real evidence, illustration supports understanding, icons guide, texture adds authorship, and organic form remains secondary to inclusion, accessibility, consent, and truth.
