# Editorial Images & Graphic Assets Guide

> A comprehensive, brand-neutral art-direction reference for editorial websites in 2026.
>
> This document defines the use of photography, illustration, collage, archives, icons, borders, separators, diagrams, charts, maps, captions, credits, video, motion, responsive media, accessibility, rights, and AI-generated assets. Use it with `editorial-web-design-system-2026.md`.

---

## 1. Purpose

Editorial imagery is not filler. It may provide:

- evidence;
- identity;
- atmosphere;
- explanation;
- narrative sequence;
- historical context;
- visual pause;
- emotional focus;
- navigation;
- documentation.

A strong editorial visual system distinguishes clearly between these roles.

```text
Evidence
> explanation
> navigation
> atmosphere
```

Decorative graphics must never be presented as documentary evidence.

## 2. Editorial visual directions

Select one primary art direction.

### 2.1 Documentary

- truthful context;
- real people and environments;
- restrained grading;
- visible process and place;
- accurate captions and credits.

### 2.2 Magazine art direction

- deliberate styling;
- strong composition;
- expressive crops;
- visual series;
- controlled lighting;
- cover-like feature images.

### 2.3 Cultural and curatorial

- artworks, artifacts, archives, exhibitions, and installation context;
- careful reproduction standards;
- object metadata;
- neutral documentation mixed with selected interpretive images.

### 2.4 Mixed-media editorial

- photography;
- collage;
- scanned material;
- handwriting;
- illustration;
- diagrams;
- type as image.

The layer system must remain coherent.

### 2.5 Data-led editorial

- charts;
- maps;
- diagrams;
- annotated evidence;
- source notes;
- interactive explanatory graphics.

### 2.6 Premium editorial

- exceptional photography;
- restrained graphics;
- refined captions;
- strong image scale;
- controlled sequencing;
- minimal but precise iconography.

## 4. Photography principles

### 4.1 Art direction before filters

Editorial authenticity depends on:

- subject selection;
- access;
- moment;
- environment;
- camera position;
- light;
- sequence;
- crop;
- context;
- caption.

A generic image does not become editorial merely through monochrome grading.

### 4.2 Create image stories

For important features, plan a series:

```text
Establishing view
Medium contextual view
Action
Detail
Portrait
Transition image
Closing image
```

A series creates pacing and evidence that a single hero image cannot provide.

### 4.3 Preserve truth

Do not manipulate documentary photographs in ways that alter factual meaning.

Avoid:

- removing relevant people or objects;
- adding elements;
- changing product or clothing details;
- creating a misleading crop;
- presenting a reenactment as an observed event;
- using generated imagery as a photograph of a real event.

Basic tonal, crop, and color corrections may be appropriate when they do not change meaning.

### 4.4 Human-centered 2026 direction

Current visual direction favors images that feel authored, emotionally credible, and less artificially perfect. Use candid behavior, real environments, natural gestures, and context where appropriate. Authenticity should come from access and judgment, not simulated film damage.

## 5. Photography categories

## 5.1 Documentary photography

Use for:

- reporting;
- real operations;
- community stories;
- field work;
- events;
- institutions;
- social and environmental subjects.

Rules:

- identify who, what, where, and when;
- preserve contextual details;
- caption accurately;
- document consent and usage rights;
- avoid generic stock substitutions;
- distinguish posed portraits from observed scenes.

## 5.2 Editorial portraiture

Define a portrait system:

- camera height;
- lens character;
- background;
- lighting;
- crop;
- expression;
- styling;
- color treatment;
- aspect ratios.

Recommended deliverables:

```text
4:5 or 3:4 formal portrait
3:2 environmental portrait
1:1 directory crop
16:9 contextual feature crop
```

Avoid inconsistent head size, lighting, and background across a contributor series.

## 5.3 Still life and object photography

Use for:

- products;
- books;
- artifacts;
- tools;
- documents;
- material culture;
- food;
- fashion details.

Rules:

- preserve accurate color where relevant;
- use consistent surface and lighting logic;
- include scale when useful;
- provide detail views;
- avoid decorative styling that misrepresents the object.

## 5.4 Architecture and place

Use a sequence of:

- exterior context;
- approach;
- wide interior;
- human scale;
- material detail;
- activity;
- environmental relationship.

Correct strong perspective only when it improves representation without falsifying space.

## 5.5 Event photography

Provide:

- venue context;
- audience;
- speaker or performer;
- interaction;
- detail;
- atmosphere;
- closing or outcome.

Do not use only podium or stage images.

## 6. Composition and negative space

Editorial images should be commissioned or selected for the intended layout.

Define:

```text
Text-left negative space
Text-right negative space
Centered subject
Edge subject
No overlay
Full bleed
Inset image
Vertical feature
Panoramic feature
```

### Rules

- Do not assume any photograph can support overlaid text.
- Keep faces and meaningful objects away from unsafe crop zones.
- Prepare separate mobile crops.
- Align subjects or horizon lines with the editorial grid where appropriate.
- Use visual tension deliberately, not accidentally.
- Maintain a clear focal point.

---

## 7. Crop system

Define a limited ratio family.

Suggested editorial ratios:

```text
Lead / panorama: 16:9, 2:1, or 21:9
Feature landscape: 3:2
Standard landscape: 4:3
Portrait: 4:5 or 3:4
Square: 1:1
Archive document: original ratio
Social preview: 1.91:1 and 1:1
```

### Crop rules

- Use the same ratio for comparable modules.
- Do not crop artworks or documents arbitrarily.
- Preserve captions and credit relationship.
- Avoid automatic center crop when the subject is off-center.
- Record crop focal points in the CMS.
- Use `<picture>` for art-directed mobile variants.

## 9. Captions and credits

Captions are editorial content.

A caption may provide:

- identification;
- context;
- location;
- date;
- explanation;
- relationship to the argument;
- credit;
- rights note.

### Caption rules

- Place captions directly with media.
- Do not repeat the alt text verbatim.
- Keep captions readable and high contrast.
- Distinguish caption from credit.
- Use consistent punctuation.
- Identify composite, archival, generated, or illustrative material.
- Do not hide essential context only in hover.

### Credit rules

Potential credit structure:

```text
Photograph: Name / Organization
Illustration: Name
Courtesy: Archive or owner
Source: Dataset or publication
```

Follow contractual wording exactly.

## 10. Alternative text

Alt text and captions have different functions.

### Informative photograph

Describe the useful visual information in context.

### Decorative image

Use empty alt text.

### Portrait

Usually identify the person when identity is relevant. Do not describe sensitive or irrelevant physical attributes.

### Chart or diagram

Provide a concise summary plus a nearby structured explanation or table.

### Artwork

Alt text should identify work and relevant visual content, while extended description may be needed for interpretation.

### Rules

- Do not begin with “image of” unless the medium matters.
- Do not copy a long caption into alt text.
- Do not omit meaningful visual evidence.
- Do not use filenames as alt text.
- Keep decorative visual marks hidden from assistive technology.

## 11. Color grading

Define one or more approved treatments by asset role.

### Natural documentary

- accurate white balance;
- restrained saturation;
- preserved shadow and highlight detail;
- realistic skin;
- consistent contrast.

### Editorial neutral

- slightly stronger tonal separation;
- controlled palette;
- clean highlights;
- modest color bias.

### Monochrome

Use when:

- source images vary widely;
- archival relationship matters;
- a publication requires a strong visual system;
- color would distract from form or subject.

Do not use monochrome to make unrelated stock feel authentic.

### Duotone or spot color

Appropriate for:

- issue identity;
- section covers;
- explanatory features;
- archival unification;
- promotional graphics.

Avoid applying it to evidence that requires accurate color.

## 12. Archival material

Archive assets may include:

- photographs;
- letters;
- manuscripts;
- posters;
- newspapers;
- maps;
- books;
- tickets;
- technical drawings;
- audio and film stills.

### Rules

- Preserve original ratio.
- Record source, date, creator, and rights.
- Do not remove institutional marks without permission.
- Provide transcription for important handwritten or printed text.
- Use high-resolution zoom only when needed.
- Distinguish original object color from editorial tinting.
- Avoid fake aging.
- Do not assume old material is public domain.

### Display patterns

- neutral document viewer;
- full-bleed crop with access to full object;
- side-by-side transcription;
- before/after comparison;
- chronological sequence;
- annotated detail.

## 13. Illustration

Editorial illustration may:

- interpret an abstract topic;
- protect identity;
- visualize an impossible scene;
- create publication personality;
- summarize a complex issue;
- provide a cover image;
- support satire or opinion;
- explain a process.

### Illustration directions

- conceptual editorial;
- geometric;
- hand-drawn;
- engraving;
- collage;
- technical;
- painterly;
- data-informed;
- 3D or spatial;
- typographic.

### Rules

- Use one coherent family per series or publication area.
- Define stroke, fill, texture, perspective, and detail.
- Credit the illustrator.
- Label illustration when it could be mistaken for documentation.
- Avoid generic vector-library scenes.
- Preserve exact wording outside generated image text.
- Create simplified variants for small cards.

## 14. Collage and mixed media

A collage should use a deliberate layer structure.

Example:

```text
Base field
Primary photographic subject
Secondary archive fragment
Graphic shape
Type label
Annotation
Texture
```

### Rules

- Limit material types.
- Preserve focal hierarchy.
- Keep evidence distinguishable from decorative fragments.
- Avoid random tape, torn paper, and photocopy effects.
- Use consistent shadow or edge logic.
- Test responsive recomposition.
- Keep meaningful text as HTML where possible.
- Record rights for every source element.

## 15. Icons and symbols

Editorial sites need fewer icons than application interfaces.

Appropriate uses:

- search;
- menu;
- play and pause;
- audio;
- save;
- share;
- print;
- expand;
- gallery navigation;
- content type;
- external link.

### Icon-system rules

- Use one functional family.
- Define grid, stroke, caps, joins, and optical size.
- Pair unfamiliar icons with labels.
- Do not decorate every article card with an icon.
- Distinguish content-type symbols from interaction icons.
- Provide accessible names for functional icons.
- Hide decorative marks from assistive technology.

Suggested base:

```text
24 × 24 viewBox
1.5–2 px stroke
One global cap and join system
16, 20, and 24 px rendered sizes
```

## 16. Borders, frames, and separators

Editorial graphic systems often use:

- horizontal rules;
- vertical column rules;
- inset frames;
- image keylines;
- double rules;
- chapter bars;
- numbered separators;
- crop or registration marks;
- issue-color edges.

### Rules

- Use whitespace before adding a line.
- Keep line weights consistent.
- Align to grid and captions.
- Do not frame every image.
- Use stronger rules for section change, lighter rules for local grouping.
- Ensure meaningful boundaries remain visible.
- Avoid borders that imitate form fields.

### Signature separator options

Choose no more than one primary signature:

- section number crossing a rule;
- short heavy bar plus long hairline;
- issue-color vertical rule;
- folio marker;
- aligned dot or square marker;
- label inset into horizontal line.

## 17. Typographic graphics

Editorial graphics may be made from:

- headlines;
- numerals;
- punctuation;
- quotations;
- indexes;
- dates;
- initials;
- chapter numbers;
- annotations.

Rules:

- Keep exact meaningful copy as live text.
- Do not rasterize headings merely for effect.
- Test localization and line wrapping.
- Use decorative letters as atmosphere only when they do not duplicate essential text.
- Ensure motion has a static alternative.

## 18. Data visualization

Editorial data graphics should answer a clear question.

### Suitable forms

- bar chart for comparison;
- line chart for time;
- scatter plot for relationship;
- map for meaningful geography;
- timeline for sequence;
- annotated table for exact values;
- small multiples for repeated comparison;
- diagram for systems and processes.

### Rules

- Include source, period, unit, and update date.
- Do not invent data.
- Use direct labels.
- Avoid 3D effects.
- Explain uncertainty and missing data.
- Provide a table or text equivalent.
- Use annotation to explain significance, not restate values.
- Preserve consistent category colors.

---

## 19. Maps

Use maps when location is essential to the story.

Rules:

- Use an appropriate projection.
- Avoid irrelevant basemap detail.
- Label important locations directly.
- Provide text or table equivalent.
- Do not rely on hover.
- Explain aggregation.
- Distinguish disputed boundaries where relevant.
- Include source and date.
- Avoid decorative world maps that only imply scale.

---

## 20. Diagrams and explanatory graphics

Use diagrams for:

- process;
- system relationship;
- anatomy;
- flow;
- sequence;
- comparison;
- hierarchy;
- cause and effect.

### Rules

- Give each diagram one primary message.
- Limit node shapes.
- Use consistent connector meaning.
- Avoid crossing lines.
- Label directly.
- Create a simplified mobile version.
- Provide text explanation.
- Keep exact data and labels editable.
- Use SVG where practical.

---

## 21. Video and audio

Editorial media may include:

- documentary video;
- interview;
- animation;
- ambient footage;
- audio story;
- podcast;
- archival clip;
- explainer.

### Rules

- Do not autoplay audio.
- Provide captions and transcripts.
- Use a meaningful poster image.
- Show duration.
- Include source and rights.
- Keep playback controls visible.
- Support keyboard operation.
- Do not make essential article information available only in media.
- Respect reduced-motion and data-saving preferences.
- Avoid persistent floating video that blocks reading.

---

## 22. Scrollytelling and visual essays

Visual storytelling may use:

- sticky media stage;
- chapter captions;
- map progression;
- image sequence;
- data transition;
- annotated object;
- before and after;
- spatial movement.

### Requirements

- The story must have a clear narrative arc.
- Users retain normal scroll control.
- Progress is visible.
- A reduced-motion version exists.
- The content remains understandable on mobile.
- Text is available outside canvas or video.
- Long media loads progressively.
- Users can skip or exit.
- Important evidence has captions and sources.

Do not use scrollytelling simply to make a standard article feel expensive.

## 23. Responsive media delivery

Use responsive sources.

```html
<picture>
  <source
    media="(max-width: 48rem)"
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/media/feature-mobile-640.avif 640w,
            /media/feature-mobile-960.avif 960w"
    sizes="100vw">
  <source
    type="image/avif"
    srcset="/media/feature-960.avif 960w,
            /media/feature-1440.avif 1440w,
            /media/feature-1920.avif 1920w"
    sizes="(min-width: 92rem) 92rem, 100vw">
  <img
    src="/media/feature-1440.webp"
    width="1920"
    height="1280"
    alt="Specific alternative text"
    decoding="async">
</picture>
```

### Rules

- Prioritize lead media.
- Lazy-load below-fold assets.
- Specify intrinsic dimensions.
- Use AVIF or WebP for photography.
- Use SVG for line graphics and icons.
- Preserve PNG only when needed.
- Do not ship original camera or scan resolution.
- Test grain, fine lines, and text after compression.
- Avoid large animated GIFs.

## 25. Rights, consent, and provenance

Never assume an image is reusable because it is online or old.

Record:

- photographer or creator;
- archive or owner;
- license;
- territory;
- duration;
- allowed modifications;
- required credit wording;
- model or property release where relevant;
- sensitive-use restrictions;
- AI-generation or editing history.

### Generated imagery

Generated images must not be presented as:

- real reporting;
- real people;
- real facilities;
- historical evidence;
- completed work;
- authentic archive material.

Label internally and externally when context requires it.

## 26. AI image-generation prompt structure

```text
Editorial [photograph / illustration / collage] in [specific visual direction].
Subject: [specific subject and action].
Context: [credible location and situation].
Lighting: [natural, studio, direct flash, etc.].
Composition: [camera position, focal point, negative-space direction].
Palette: [limited palette or natural color].
Page role: [lead feature, portrait, card, cover, explanatory graphic].
Aspect ratio: [ratio].
Include: [required period, objects, clothing, environment].
Exclude: fake logos, illegible text, generic stock behavior, unrelated visual eras,
excessive effects, false documentary cues.
```

### AI rules

- Do not request exact important text inside the image.
- Do not imitate a living artist's style as the sole instruction.
- Avoid fake photojournalism.
- Review anatomy, objects, signs, and historical details.
- Use generated imagery primarily for illustration, concept, atmosphere, or clearly labeled reconstruction.

## 27. Common failures

Avoid:

- generic stock photography presented as editorial evidence;
- one monochrome preset on every asset;
- captions separated from images;
- missing creator credit;
- random crop ratios;
- automatic center crops;
- generated images presented as real events;
- decorative charts with invented data;
- maps without source or date;
- mixed icon families;
- borders around every image;
- excessive collage texture;
- text baked into feature images;
- autoplay video;
- visual stories without reduced-motion fallback;
- archive scans without transcription;
- inaccessible hover-only annotations;
- unlicensed artwork or logos.

## 28. AI-agent instructions

An AI agent preparing editorial visual assets must:

1. identify whether the asset is evidence, explanation, navigation, or atmosphere;
2. select a coherent visual direction;
3. define image roles and crop ratios;
4. prepare desktop and mobile crops;
5. define caption and credit requirements;
6. preserve factual accuracy;
7. distinguish photography, illustration, archive, and generated material;
8. define icon and separator systems;
9. create accessible alternatives for diagrams and charts;
10. record rights and provenance;
11. optimize formats and dimensions;
12. avoid false documentary representation;
13. provide static and reduced-motion variants;
14. use actual source data for graphics;
15. validate the sequence as well as individual images.

The agent must not:

- invent attribution;
- invent licenses;
- fabricate visual evidence;
- treat all assets with one preset;
- use random graphic effects;
- place critical exact content only in an image;
- create inaccessible or misleading data graphics.

## 31. Final rule

Editorial visuals must do more than make a page attractive. Photography should document, illustration should interpret, diagrams should explain, icons should guide, borders should organize, and every asset should have a clear source, role, and relationship to the story.
